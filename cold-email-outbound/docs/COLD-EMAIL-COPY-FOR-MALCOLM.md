## 1b. Who we're targeting + how we pitch (Eric's ICP, locked 2026-06-29)

Eric defined the ideal seller. Write to THIS person.

**Who we want**
- **Franchise Sellers:** franchisees of established systems (the brand has ~50 to 1,000+ locations), with a business doing **$1–5M in revenue** and **5+ employees**. Over $5M is too big for us.
- **Company Sellers:** independent owners in **home services, home health** (Eric's favorite), service businesses, wholesale distribution, light manufacturing, and auto service. **$1–10M in revenue**, in business 3+ years. We do NOT target construction, education, retail, or real-estate-heavy businesses (car washes, storage, dealerships).
- **Both:** profitable. Revenue over **$1M** is the line Eric cares about most.

**The angle that lands (Eric's word): trust.**
- We have a **real, proven process**, **28 years** doing this, **nationwide**.
- **Our fee model is aligned with the owner:** a **small upfront onboarding fee, and the bulk of our fee comes only when we actually sell the business.** Do NOT write "only paid if we sell" — that is not accurate. Frame it as: most of what we earn depends on getting them sold, so we are on the same side.
- We **work alongside their existing accountant, attorney, and advisors**, not around them.

**Objections to disarm (Eric):**
- They don't know the process, or think they have too much / too little time.
- They think they **already have a buyer** (a son, daughter, or key employee). Usually they don't.
- Many **don't even know a business broker exists** — they assume they'd just go to their accountant or attorney. Awareness is half the battle.

**What makes them ready (use as gentle context, never pressure):** retirement, burnout, a health event or death, divorce, a partnership split, market timing, or an unsolicited offer landing in their lap.

The targeting rules above are now live in the qualification engine. What you sharpen in the copy is the per-segment *language* (the words a home-health owner uses are not the words a franchisee uses).

## 3. What's automatic (don't worry about it)

The system handles the plumbing. You write the words once in the template; it does the rest per recipient. You do **not** hand-edit these per person.

- **Name, company, industry merge.** `{{firstName}}`, `{{company}}`, etc. fill in automatically from our data. If a record has no first name it falls back to a generic but readable version, so nothing breaks.
- **Light wording variation for deliverability.** The system rotates a few opening words on the final email so 10,000 outgoing copies aren't byte-for-byte identical (spam filters dislike that). It's automatic and stable.
- **The offer line / call-to-action.** The "here's what we can do for you" sentence (the free opinion-of-value offer) is inserted by the system, not typed into each template.
- **The legal footer.** Every email auto-gets our physical address and an opt-out line at the bottom. This is legally required (CAN-SPAM). **Leave it alone. It must stay on every send.**

One thing to know: any **misspelled merge tag prints literally** in the email. If a template ever says `{{frist_name}}`, that exact text would ship. So if you spot a `{{double-brace}}` thing that looks misspelled in the preview, flag it.

The only thing that isn't automatic and **is** yours: the actual sentences in each email, the subject lines, and the wording of the offer/CTA.

## 4. Our voice rules

These are your rules. Here's how they apply specifically to cold email.

- **No em dashes.** Use periods or commas. (This is the single biggest fix needed in the current drafts; see Section 7.)
- **No random bolding.** Cold email should look like a plain note from a person, never a formatted marketing blast.
- **Short, direct sentences.** Especially the first email. Aim for under ~80 words. Each follow-up should be shorter than the one before it.
- **One primary call-to-action per email.** One ask, low-friction ("Worth a quick look?" / "Want to chat next week?"). Not two asks stacked back-to-back.
- **Reads like a real person typed it.** No corporate filler ("leverage," "synergy," "I hope this email finds you well").
- **Keep unconfirmed stats qualitative.** If we reference buyer demand, say "hundreds of buyer inquiries each month," not a specific number, until Theodore confirms the figure.

## 5. What a great cold email does

Run each email through this checklist. If any line fails, it needs a revision.

**The first email starts a conversation. It does not sell.**
- [ ] Soft opener or light question, not a pitch and not a wall of offer text
- [ ] Under ~80 words
- [ ] One clear, low-friction ask (reply / quick question)
- [ ] Uses *this* audience's actual language (a franchisee's concerns are not an independent owner's concerns)

**Across the whole sequence:**
- [ ] No em dashes, no random bolding, no corporate filler
- [ ] Each follow-up is shorter than the last
- [ ] The final email is a soft, seller-appropriate breakup ("no rush, if now isn't the time I'll check back"). Never the SaaS-style "can you point me to the right person?" (the owner *is* the right person)
- [ ] Any proof point or claim is true and, ideally, specific
- [ ] Subject line is honest about who we are and why we're writing, with no fake "quick favor" pretext

**Why the cadence is what it is.** Selling a business is a once-in-a-lifetime, emotional decision, not a software trial. So the sequence is deliberately gentle: 3 emails spaced 1, 4, then 6 days apart. Resist the urge to make it more aggressive.

## 6. Avoid these (AI-tells and spam triggers)

Concrete things that make copy read like a robot or trip spam filters:

- **Em dashes** (the long dash). The most common AI tell, and against our rules. Replace with periods or commas.
- **"It's not X, it's Y"** constructions.
- **Random bolding** for drama or emphasis.
- **Corporate filler:** "leverage," "synergy," "I hope this email finds you well," "circle back," "touch base."
- **The same generic copy across both brands.** A franchise resale and an independent business sale are different worlds. The words should be different.
- **Two CTAs or a hard close** in one email.
- **The SaaS referral breakup** ("point me to the right person"). The sole owner is the contact.
- **A fake personalization detail.** Never invent an "I saw you recently..." hook. If we don't have a real signal, the system leaves it out; that's correct, don't paper over it.
- **Misleading subject lines** ("quick favor," "pulled something for you"). For a brokerage this borders on deceptive. Keep subjects honest.
- **Unverifiable result claims** stated as fact ("sold for more than they expected") with no proof or number behind them.

## 7. Specific things to look at in the current drafts

A concrete starting to-do, organized by brand and stream. Three streams are live for review.

### Across ALL THREE streams (do these first)

1. **Strip the em dashes.** Every template uses real em dashes, which breaks our top voice rule. They sit in the first-email proof line and at the start of every final email. Quick find-and-replace, long dash to a period or comma. **This must happen before any live send.**
2. **Decide on the "result" claim.** All three lean on a version of *"helped another owner sell for more than they expected."* It's specific-sounding but unprovable, and it reads like every other cold email. Consider swapping it for a credibility statement we can stand behind (process, confidentiality, experience) rather than an outcome promise.
3. **Differentiate the subject lines.** All three streams reuse near-identical subjects ("Quick question {{firstName}}," "Thoughts {{firstName}}"). These are heavily overused and don't distinguish the brands or segments. The strongest ones are the specific exit-themed subjects ("thinking about what's next?", "a quick one about {{company}}"). The generic two are the weakest. Worth giving each stream its own voice and A/B testing.

### FS · Niche (franchisees of a specific named system)

- **Protect and verify the system name.** This stream's whole advantage is naming the prospect's franchise system in the opener ("we specialize in *FirstLight Home Care* resales specifically"). If the clean system name isn't passed in correctly, it can render a messy full company string instead ("...FirstLight Home Care of Boise resales specifically"). Confirm in the preview pack that a clean name actually slots in.
- The third subject ("a quick one about {{company}}") is the strongest of the three. The first two are weak.

### FS · Broad (franchise owners, any system)

- **The "28 years" credential is confirmed, use it.** The opener cites our broker president's years in the business. Eric's ICP puts it at **28 years** (Theodore locked this 2026-06-29; supersedes the earlier "27"). 28 is correct to ship. A rounder "25 years" is also accurate if it reads better. Pick whichever lands.
- This is the broadest, least-personalized stream by design (no system name). Decide whether a fully generic first email is acceptable, or whether it needs one genuinely specific hook.
- The casual CTA ("Worth a quick look?") is good, but it follows a long, formal offer sentence. Tighten the offer line so the tone matches.

### CS · Company Sellers (independent owners by industry)

- **Most em dashes of the three; clean them all.**
- **Sanity-check the industry merge.** The opener says "another owner in {{industry}}" using the raw industry value, lowercased. "another owner in home care" reads fine, but "another owner in hvac services" looks like a typo (lowercased acronym), and a blank value reads awkwardly. Worth a quick look at what industry labels actually exist in the CS list with whoever owns that data.
- **Vary the word "value."** The sequence repeats "value" a lot ("value and sell," "opinion of value," "complimentary opinion of value"). It starts to read like keyword stuffing. Mix in "worth," "valuation," "what it could sell for."

## 7b. Broker best-practices essentials (the short version of the Playbook)

The [full Playbook](BROKER-COPY-PLAYBOOK.md) has the detail and the swipe library. The essentials for this audience, which is different from generic cold email because selling a business is a once-in-a-lifetime, emotional, confidential decision:

- **Lead with the owner's curiosity, not the sale.** Make the implied ask "do you know what it's worth?" not "do you want to sell?" Only about 15% of owners have ever had a valuation, so the worth question is live for almost everyone, while the sell question triggers defenses.
- **Credibility beats outcome claims.** Trust is the biggest driver of replies here. Swap "sold for more than they expected" for what we can prove: 28 years, confidentiality, our process. This is the #1 fix.
- **Name confidentiality early.** An owner's first fear is that staff, customers, or competitors find out. One line ("stays completely between us, nothing happens without your say-so") defuses it.
- **Frame the OOV honestly.** A free, no-obligation broker's market opinion, "not a formal appraisal," yours to keep whether you ever sell or not. Never promise an exact or guaranteed value.
- **Timing, not interest, is the blocker.** Most owners are 2 to 7 years out. A "not yet" is the start of a multi-year relationship, so give them the "plan early gives you options" reframe and a soft breakup that keeps the door open.
- **Never** say "I have a buyer for your business." That exact line now reads as acquisition phishing and torches credibility on sight.
- **One soft question per email, no link in email 1.** Soft asks reply about 3x better than a meeting ask. Send a booking link only after a positive reply.
- **FS and CS are different worlds.** A franchisee thinks about system rules and resale approval. An independent owner thinks about multiples and succession. Never ship identical copy across both brands.
